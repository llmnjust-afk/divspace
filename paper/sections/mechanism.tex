\section{What Ablation Establishes, and What It Does Not}
\label{sec:mechanism}

\paragraph{Removal and addition are not symmetric.}
Injection asks whether adding a direction increases diversity; ablation asks whether removing it
decreases diversity. The two can come apart, and in our models they do. Projecting the response
subspace out of the residual stream at an early layer collapses diversity dramatically, while
adding the same subspace does nothing. \Cref{tab:ablation} gives the clearest case. In pythia-1b,
ablating the fitted direction at layer 4 drops \vendi{} from a baseline of $6.13$ to $1.35$, and the
effect is graded with depth: $4.00$ at layer 8 and $5.07$ at layer 12. That gradient suggests early layers establish the variability
later layers preserve or collapse.

\paragraph{The collapse is not direction-specific.}
Ablating a random vector at the same layer leaves \vendi{} at $6.05$ against a baseline of $6.13$,
so the collapse is not an artifact of perturbing the stream; it requires removing the right subspace.
The control that decides the interpretation is the shuffled arm, where the activations are unchanged
and only the pairing between dispersion scores and responses is destroyed. It collapses diversity
almost as completely as the real direction: $1.46$ at layer 4 and $3.67$ at layer 8. Keeping the
activations while discarding the contrast therefore suffices to reproduce the effect, so the
contrast contributes essentially nothing; what matters is that the removed subspace spans the
responses themselves, which destroys the model's ability to branch within a prompt.

\paragraph{Ablation across models is null.}
The pythia-1b result is the exception rather than the rule. Across the four models of the first
ablation sweep, eleven of twelve comparisons against placebo ablations cross zero, and the twelfth
points against the hypothesis: on pythia-2.8b the fitted ablation \emph{raises} \vendi{} relative to
the shuffled control by $+0.191$ ($[-0.307,-0.074]$ when signed in the direction of the hypothesis).
The effect in \Cref{tab:ablation} is therefore a property of one model at one depth, and we report it
as such.

\paragraph{Why the asymmetry matters.}
If diversity were a scalar read out along a direction, adding it should raise the value and removing
it should lower it. We observe the second and not the first, which is not a ceiling effect: lowering
the temperature to $0.4$ moves pythia-2.8b's baseline to $4.7$ and creates metric headroom, yet
injection still produced no effect in any of the nine low-temperature comparisons. A pure ceiling
account predicts a positive effect there; an account in which the estimator is noise predicts none,
and that is what we observe.
